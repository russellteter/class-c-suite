# Turnaround Operating Library

A reference layer for the Strategic AI Operating Model. This is what an expert turnaround COO would have memorized and use as the substrate for every decision. Future Claude should cite from this explicitly during Pass 1 bootstrap and any deep investigation.

Class context anchor: ARR $35.85M sliding to ~$20.57M over 16 months, weekly cash trough of $111,766 on July 26, 2026, $30M Barclays facility (term + revolver), preferred stack zeroed via Holdco recap, 41-person GTM roster, edtech SaaS in late-stage decline, COO transition in progress. References to "Class" below assume this state.

## Section 1: Strategic Framing Frameworks

### Andy Grove — Strategic Inflection Point (Only the Paranoid Survive)
Grove's central insight: businesses don't die from one bad quarter; they die because a "10X force" changes the rules and management keeps running the old playbook. The inflection point is the moment where the curve of your business actually breaks — where the old strategy no longer produces the old result. Grove's hardest lesson is that the inflection point is most visible to people at the edges (sales reps, support engineers) and most invisible to senior management, which is invested in the prior strategy. The job at the top is to listen for the dissonance, then act before the data fully confirms it.

For Class: the inflection point has already happened. The 10X force is the collapse of the synchronous-video-LMS category as a defensible niche — Zoom and Teams have commoditized the underlying tech, Canvas and Anthology have absorbed the workflow, and AI-native platforms have started rewriting the whole experience. The ARR slide from $35.85M to $20.57M isn't a slow-bleed; it's the inflection-point curve playing out in real time. Russell's role is to stop debating whether the inflection happened and start operating on the assumption that it did. Every plan that assumes Class returns to growth in its current form is a denial artifact.

### Hamilton Helmer — 7 Powers (Scale Economies, Network Economies, Counter-Positioning, Switching Costs, Branding, Cornered Resource, Process Power)
Helmer's framework is the most rigorous answer to "do you actually have a moat?" Most companies confuse temporary advantages (a good product, a fast team, a cheap CAC moment) with Powers, which are *structural* and *persistent*. Of the seven, only Switching Costs and Process Power are typically available to mid-stage SaaS without category leadership.

For Class: be honest. Class has weak-to-moderate Switching Costs (LMS integrations, faculty training, custom configurations) and possibly some Process Power in Higher Ed sales motion. It has no Scale Economies (small relative to Zoom/Teams), no Network Economies (synchronous video isn't a network), no Counter-Positioning against incumbents (incumbents can copy), no Branding power outside a narrow EDU bubble, and no Cornered Resource. The strategic implication: the only defensible playbook is to lean *hard* into Switching Costs — make Class the deepest-integrated, hardest-to-rip-out piece of the institutional stack for a concentrated segment — and abandon any strategy that assumes new-logo growth in open competition with Zoom or Canvas.

### Clayton Christensen — Disruption Theory
Christensen distinguished sustaining innovation (better products for existing customers, which incumbents win) from disruptive innovation (worse-but-cheaper or different-axis products that start in low-end or new markets and move up). The trap: incumbents rationally ignore disruptors because their margins and customers say to. Then the disruptor moves upmarket and the incumbent has no answer.

For Class: Class is being disrupted from two directions simultaneously. From above, the synchronous-video commodity (Zoom/Teams) keeps moving down into education-specific workflows that Class used to own. From below, AI-native platforms (Khanmigo, Magic School, the next generation) are reframing the entire "what is a class session" question — the answer may not involve synchronous video at all. Class isn't the disruptor here; Class is the incumbent being squeezed. The brutal honest read: Class's product is sustaining-innovation against a category that's being disrupted out from under it. Strategy must either (a) become the disruptor in a smaller, defensible adjacent market, or (b) extract maximum value from the current asset before the curve runs out.

### McKinsey Three Horizons — Defend (H1), Build (H2), Seed (H3)
Three Horizons is a portfolio frame: H1 is current core (defend cash), H2 is emerging businesses (build to scale), H3 is options (seed and learn). The common failure is companies in H1 decline pretending they have H2 businesses when they have only H1 extensions.

For Class: in cash crisis, Three Horizons collapses to two questions. (1) How do we extract maximum H1 cash and survive? (2) Do we have a real H2, or are we kidding ourselves? CoSo and AI-feature integrations may look like H2, but if they require capital we don't have and time we don't have, they are H3 daydreams, not H2 bets. The disciplined move is to ruthlessly classify every initiative, kill the H3 charity cases, double down on one H2 if there is one, and stop pretending H1 will reaccelerate.

### BCG Growth-Share Matrix — Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks, Dogs
The matrix is mostly overrated as strategy (market share and growth rate aren't the only dimensions that matter), but it is *useful* as a forced-portfolio conversation. The discipline is: name every product line, plot it, and ask whether you're funding the matrix correctly.

For Class: Class core (sync video + EDU workflow) is a Cash Cow in decline — milk it, do not invest in it. CoSo is probably a Question Mark or Dog depending on whether anyone will pay for it. Integrations and AI features are Question Marks. There are no Stars. The matrix-driven decision: extract from the Cash Cow, kill the Dogs, pick at most one Question Mark to invest in. Resist the temptation to "rebalance the portfolio" — in a cash crisis, you cut, you don't rebalance.

### Jim Collins — Stockdale Paradox
Admiral Stockdale survived seven years in a POW camp by holding two truths at once: absolute faith that he would prevail in the end, *and* unflinching honesty about the brutal facts of the present. The optimists who said "we'll be out by Christmas" died because each disappointed Christmas broke them. The realists who lost hope died too. Only the paradox-holders survived.

For Class: this is the operating discipline for Russell personally and for the leadership team. The brutal facts: ARR is falling, cash is tight, the July trough is real, the category is in decline. The faith: there is a path through this that preserves enterprise value, protects employees, and creates optionality. Communicate both. Never sugarcoat the facts in the board deck; never lose conviction in the path. Beware of leaders who can only do one side — pure optimists are dangerous in turnarounds, and pure pessimists demoralize the org.

### Peter Drucker — "What business are we really in?"
Drucker's most famous forcing question. The answer is almost always not what management thinks. Railroads thought they were in railroads; they were in transportation. Class thinks it's in synchronous video education software; that's the SKU, not the business.

For Class: the real business may be "integrated EDU operations for institutions that can't afford or don't want to assemble their own stack." Or it may be "the relationship layer between faculty and the LMS." Or it may simply be "a profitable services business with software glue." Russell's job is to force this conversation in the next 30 days — without an honest answer, every other strategic move is guessing. Beware the answer that flatters the current product roadmap; the right answer usually requires cutting something the team loves.

## Section 2: Turnaround Doctrine

### Michael Watkins — The First 90 Days (and the 100-Day Plan)
Watkins' framework: STARS model (Startup, Turnaround, Accelerated growth, Realignment, Sustaining success) — different situations require different first-90-day playbooks. In a Turnaround, the priority sequence is: secure short-term survival, build a small core team you trust, identify the one or two interventions that change trajectory, and earn early wins to fund credibility for the harder later moves.

For Class: Russell's 100-day plan must front-load cash stabilization (July trough), team assessment (who stays / who goes / who gets upgraded), and one visible early win (a successful renewal, a deferred AP win, a covenant-relief conversation that lands). The temptation in a turnaround is to do everything at once. Watkins' discipline is to do the *fewest* things that move the survival metrics, and to defer the reinvention work until survival is locked.

### Cash-Is-King Discipline
In a turnaround, P&L lies, balance sheet lies, only cash tells the truth. The disciplines are: a rolling 13-week cash forecast updated weekly; DSO compression through aggressive collections; DPO extension through structured vendor renegotiation; working-capital release from inventory, prepaids, and deposits; and a clear distinction between "cash in the bank" and "cash we control."

For Class: this is already partially in place — the May 10 weekly forecast XLSX is the authoritative baseline. The work to push further: identify the top 10 AR accounts and assign named owners with weekly targets; identify the top 20 AP vendors and triage into "must pay," "can stretch," "can renegotiate"; pull restricted cash where contractually possible (the Coso-TD $3.245M and Barclays $2.5M); and make the 13-week the *only* cash document anyone references.

### Stabilize → Restructure → Reposition → Grow
The canonical turnaround sequence. Stabilize: stop the bleeding, lock cash. Restructure: cut cost structure to match the new revenue reality. Reposition: redefine what the business is and who it serves. Grow: reignite, but only from a base that can support it. The failure mode is trying to grow before you've restructured, or repositioning before you've stabilized.

For Class: today, Class is mid-Stabilize. Restructure is partially underway (Holdco recap, exits already happened). Reposition has not seriously started. Grow is a fantasy at this stage. Russell should be explicit with the board about which phase Class is in and resist any pressure to skip steps — boards under stress often want the "grow" narrative because it feels better, and good COOs refuse to deliver it prematurely.

### Negotiation Under Pressure — Stuart Diamond ("Getting More") and Chris Voss ("Never Split the Difference")
Forget the "negotiate from weakness" framing — that's a posture, not a method. Diamond's method: find the other side's perceptions, needs, and standards; trade things they value cheaply that you value highly. Voss's method (from FBI hostage work): tactical empathy, mirroring, labeling emotions, calibrated questions ("how am I supposed to do that?"), the "that's right" moment. Both reject hard positional bargaining.

For Class: every vendor renegotiation, every covenant-relief conversation with Barclays, every customer save conversation should use these tools. Specifically: never lead with "we're in trouble" (that's a giveaway); lead with calibrated questions about the *other side's* constraints. With Barclays, the move is to position the conversation as joint problem-solving ("how do we both come out of this whole?") rather than as a beg. With major vendors, identify what they value (long-term relationship, payment certainty, public reference) that costs Class nothing.

### The Burning Platform Communication Doctrine
Originally from John Kotter / change-management literature. The principle: people don't change because of opportunity; they change because the status quo is more painful than the change. A leader's job in a turnaround is to *make* the burning platform visible — to communicate the brutal facts in a way that creates urgency without creating panic. Get it wrong on the side of too soft and the org sleepwalks; too hard and people leave or freeze.

For Class: the burning platform must be communicated, but in *tiered* form. The board sees the full picture. The executive team sees nearly the full picture. Senior managers see enough to make their decisions. Frontline employees see what they need to do their jobs. Customers see what affects their service. Be transparent about direction; be selective about detail. The worst posture is to pretend everything is fine — employees can smell it, and customers can read the signs (delayed product, layoffs, exec turnover).

### Cut Once, Deep
Death by a thousand cuts destroys morale, creates ongoing uncertainty, and burns leadership credibility. The disciplined turnaround principle is to cut once, cut deep enough, and never have to cut again. The math: assume your forecast is 20% optimistic, then cut to that lower number.

For Class: if cuts are coming, they should be sized to a *worse* ARR scenario than the current $20.57M floor — assume the floor is $18M and cut to that. One round, communicated clearly, with severance handled properly. The CFO severance policy (2-12 weeks by tenure, spread across normal payroll) needs to be respected operationally, but the *decision* should be one decision, not five. Note the timing constraint: severance spread across payroll means cuts decided today don't materially help the July trough, so cuts are about the medium-term burn rate, not the immediate cash issue.

### Right-Sizing Without Breaking the Business — Reed Hastings' Qualified Talent Thesis
Hastings at Netflix: "we are a team, not a family." Pay top-of-market, keep only A-players, replace anyone who's a B-player with a higher-leverage A-player. Hastings' qualified-talent thesis is controversial but it's the explicit operating doctrine of high-performance turnarounds: in decline, you cannot afford to carry passengers.

For Class: the question Russell must answer for every role is "if I were hiring this seat today knowing what I know now, would I hire this person?" If no, that's a decision to make. This is hard in a small company because relationships are real, but it is the discipline. Pair with: the people you keep should be told *clearly* that they were kept on purpose and what they're being asked to do. Survivor's guilt destroys productivity if not managed.

### The Covenant-Relief Playbook
With senior debt under stress, the work is sequenced. First: read the credit agreement carefully — what is the covenant, what is the cure period, what are the lender's remedies? Second: get ahead of the breach — call the lender *before* the test date with a forecast and a request. Third: ask for the *right* relief — covenant holiday, redefined ratios, a waiver, a forbearance, or amendment. Fourth: offer something in return — additional reporting, milestones, equity cures, a clear refinance path. Lenders hate surprises and reward transparency.

For Class: the Barclays $30M facility is the central financial relationship. The covenant package and the cure mechanics must be the most-read document in the company. The relationship with the Barclays workout team (if it has been moved there) is mission-critical. The narrative to bring is: "here is our path to $X EBITDA and $Y cash by date Z; here is what we need from you to get there; here is what we will give you in reporting and milestones." Never go in asking for charity; always go in proposing a deal.

### 13-Week Cash Forecast as the Turnaround Standard
Why 13 weeks: it's one quarter, it's short enough to be accurate, it's long enough to see the next debt service date, and it's the standard the lending community will benchmark you against. The discipline is weekly update, weekly variance to prior week, named accountability for each line. The forecast is the operating system of a cash-constrained business.

For Class: this exists and is anchored on the May 10 baseline. The improvements available: tighten the named-ownership on each AR / AP line; add scenario toggles (lever activation flags) directly to the model; integrate the AWS detail; surface the variance commentary as a one-page weekly artifact for the board.

### The "No Surprises" Board Operating Cadence
Boards forgive bad news delivered on time. Boards do not forgive bad news delivered late. The operating cadence in a turnaround is: weekly written update to the board chair, biweekly full-board summary, monthly full-board call, quarterly deep dive. Every major number change goes to the board *before* it goes anywhere external.

For Class: Russell should explicitly negotiate the cadence with the board. The discipline: every Friday a one-page update (cash, ARR, top three risks, top three asks); every other Wednesday a 30-minute structured call; one monthly deep-dive. The goal is to make the board feel informed and useful, not alarmed and intrusive.

### When to Bring in a Chief Restructuring Officer (CRO)
The CRO is a specialist — typically from Alvarez & Marsal, AlixPartners, FTI, Berkeley Research Group. Bring one in when: (a) you're heading into a formal restructuring (Chapter 11, out-of-court workout), (b) the lender requires one as a condition of forbearance, (c) the management team lacks restructuring experience and time is short, or (d) you need an independent voice to give the board cover for hard decisions.

For Class: probably *not yet*. A CRO is expensive ($75K-$200K/month), and Class isn't yet in formal restructuring. The signal to revisit: if Barclays demands one as a condition of relief, or if the board loses confidence in the management narrative. Russell should know who the candidates would be — keep a shortlist warm — but resist a premature engagement that drains cash without proportionate value.

## Section 3: SaaS-Specific Turnaround Patterns

### The Rule of 40 Inversion
The Rule of 40 (growth rate + EBITDA margin >= 40%) is the canonical SaaS health metric. When growth fails, the only path back to the Rule is margin. The math is unforgiving: at -30% growth, you need +70% EBITDA margin to be "healthy," which is impossible. The honest interpretation: in decline, you're not solving for Rule of 40, you're solving for *positive free cash flow* and *covenant compliance.*

For Class: stop reporting Rule of 40 internally — it's demoralizing and not actionable. Replace with: weekly cash, NRR, gross margin, and EBITDA-on-trailing-six-months. Run the business to those four numbers.

### Bessemer's Good / Great / Best SaaS Metrics
Bessemer's framework: at scale, Good is 100% NRR, Great is 110%, Best is 130%+. Magic Number above 1.0 means efficient growth. CAC payback under 18 months. Gross margin above 75%. Burn multiple under 1.

For Class: in late-stage decline, the Bessemer benchmarks are aspirational and mostly unreachable in the short term. The diagnostic value is in NRR — what's Class's current NRR? If it's below 90%, the business is shrinking on its existing base and no amount of new logos will save it. If it's 90-100%, there's a survivable base. The first NRR fix is gross retention (stop the leaks), not expansion.

### Net Revenue Retention as the Single Survival Metric for Mature SaaS
NRR is the most honest number a SaaS company tracks because it strips out new-logo theater. NRR > 100% means the existing base is funding the business. NRR < 100% means you're running uphill — every quarter you need to win more new logos than you lost in existing-base shrinkage, which gets exponentially harder.

For Class: if NRR isn't already the centerpiece of the operating cadence, make it so. Decompose into gross retention, expansion, contraction, and churn. Each component has a different owner and a different lever. In late-stage decline, gross retention is where the biggest near-term wins are — it's cheaper to retain than to win new, and every save shows up directly in next quarter's ARR.

### Expand vs New Logo Reallocation
Sales orgs default to chasing new logos because they're psychologically rewarding (closing! winning!) and because comp plans reward them. In late-stage decline, this is backwards. Expand revenue has 3-5x better unit economics, faster cycles, and higher win rates.

For Class: examine the comp plan immediately. If reps are paid the same for expand vs new logo dollars, you'll get new-logo bias by default. Reweight to favor expand. Consider whether 1-3 named reps should focus exclusively on the top 20 accounts for expansion and retention, leaving new-logo hunting to a smaller, sharper team.

### Pricing as the Highest-Leverage Lever — Campbell / Ramanujam
Patrick Campbell's data: pricing changes drive ~4x the impact of acquisition optimizations and ~2x the impact of retention optimizations, dollar for dollar. Madhavan Ramanujam's "Monetizing Innovation": price before you build, segment willingness-to-pay, and use packaging to capture different price points.

For Class: most edtech SaaS underprices because the buying institutions are "non-profit" and the sales team feels guilty. This is a mistake. The work: identify the top quintile of accounts by usage and revisit pricing. Identify the bottom quintile by margin and consider raising minimums or sunsetting. Introduce a clear good/better/best packaging if not already in place. Pricing is the only lever that's pure margin — every dollar of price increase falls to EBITDA.

### Land and Expand Reversal
The standard SaaS thesis (land small, expand over time) inverts in late-stage decline. When expansion stalls, "land small" becomes "land tiny and never grow" — you're acquiring CAC-expensive accounts that don't return. The reversal: stop landing accounts you can't expand. Qualify harder on the front end.

For Class: this implies tightening the ICP, killing low-fit deals earlier, and concentrating sales effort on accounts that look like the best customers. Some new-logo revenue will be foregone — that's fine, because the bad-fit logos were unprofitable anyway.

### Product-Led Growth as CAC Compression
PLG works when the product can be tried and proven without sales involvement. In edtech, this is harder (institutional buying, procurement cycles) but not impossible — free department-level pilots, free single-faculty usage, etc. The point of PLG isn't to replace sales; it's to compress CAC by having the product do part of the qualification work.

For Class: a real PLG motion is probably a 12-month build, which Class doesn't have time for. The lighter-touch version: identify any product surface that can be exposed to a free trial or freemium tier, and use it as a top-of-funnel lead generator. Cheaper than paid acquisition.

### Concentrate-Then-Decide
The most powerful late-stage SaaS playbook is to identify the most defensible customer segment, concentrate everything there, and then make the strategic decision about whether to invest in expansion later. "Try to be all things to all people" is the death sentence of mid-stage SaaS.

For Class: which segment of customers loves Class the most, has the highest NRR, the lowest churn, the most stickiness? That's the core. Everything else is optional. The strategy work is to articulate that segment with precision (institution type, use case, decision-maker persona, value driver) and then realign product, sales, marketing, and CS around it. This is the *most important* strategic question for Class in the next 90 days.

## Section 4: Specific Turnaround Case Studies

### Apple 1997 — Jobs Returns, Radical SKU Reduction
When Jobs returned, Apple was 90 days from bankruptcy with 350+ products. Jobs drew a 2x2 grid (consumer/pro x desktop/portable) and killed everything outside the four boxes. He also killed the Newton, cleaned up the retail strategy, and made the Microsoft investment deal that saved cash. The lesson isn't "have a vision" — it's that in a turnaround, the strategic act is *subtraction*, not addition.

The Class lesson: every product, segment, geography, and initiative is presumptively guilty until proven innocent. Russell should run an Apple-1997 exercise — draw the 2x2 (or 3x3) that defines the core, kill everything outside it. The temptation will be to "keep optionality"; resist it. Optionality without cash to fund it is just clutter.

### Netflix 2011 — Qwikster Reversal
Hastings tried to split Netflix into Qwikster (DVD) and Netflix (streaming), got crushed by customers and stock market, and reversed the decision within three weeks. The lesson is twofold: (1) strategic mistakes happen, and the cost of denying them is higher than the cost of reversing them, (2) communicating the reversal directly and personally ("we screwed up") preserves credibility better than spin.

The Class lesson: if a prior decision (a product line, a pricing change, a key hire, a positioning) is wrong, reverse it openly. The board and the org will respect a clean reversal more than a slow defense of a bad decision. This is harder than it sounds because the COO usually inherited the decisions.

### Microsoft 2014 — Nadella's Culture Rewiring
Nadella didn't start with a new strategic vision; he started by changing the culture from "know-it-all" to "learn-it-all." He killed Ballmer's stack-ranking system, embraced the cloud (which Ballmer had reluctantly), made peace with iOS and Android, and rewired the operating model. The strategy followed the culture, not the other way around.

The Class lesson: culture eats strategy for breakfast, especially in turnarounds. If the culture is set up to defend the past, no new strategy will land. Russell needs to identify the cultural moves that unlock execution — likely around customer-obsession, financial discipline, and AI fluency — and make those moves visibly before announcing strategy shifts.

### Domino's 2009 — Admitting the Product Was Bad
Domino's faced declining sales and ran market research that said their pizza was actually rated lower than competitors. Instead of spinning it, they made a TV campaign that aired the customer criticism verbatim and announced a complete recipe reformulation. Sales recovered, stock 10x'd over the following decade.

The Class lesson: if the product has real weaknesses, the corporate posture should not be defensive. Acknowledge, fix, and tell the market about the fix. This is especially powerful for retention conversations — calling a customer to say "we know X has been a problem, here's what we're doing" outperforms any save-tactic.

### Best Buy under Hubert Joly — Renew Blue and the Human-Side Restructuring
Joly took over a Best Buy that was being written off as the next Circuit City. His "Renew Blue" plan was famously human-centered: instead of mass layoffs, he engaged front-line employees, fixed the most visible customer pain points (price-matching Amazon, fixing the website), and gradually restructured. The lesson: turnaround doesn't have to be all cuts; engaging employees as part of the solution can unlock execution speed that pure cost-cutting destroys.

The Class lesson: be wary of "slash and burn" as the default mental model. The people who do the work usually know what's wrong and have ideas. Joly-style listening tours, structured employee input on the turnaround plan, and visible early-win execution build trust that compounds. Russell's instinct to listen before cutting is consistent with this playbook.

### IBM under Gerstner — "The Last Thing IBM Needs Is a Vision"
Gerstner's famous line, from "Who Says Elephants Can't Dance." He was greeted as a CEO who would announce a grand strategic vision; he refused, saying the urgent need was operating discipline — make products work, restructure the sales model, hold P&L accountability. The strategic vision came later, after the operating fundamentals were back.

The Class lesson: in the first six months, Russell should resist the pressure to deliver a transformative strategic vision. The pressure will come from the board, from employees who want hope, from customers who want reassurance. The right answer: "we're going to fix the fundamentals first, then earn the right to a vision." Operating discipline is the foundation; vision without it is theater.

### Adobe's Subscription Transition — The Pain of Revenue Model Change
Adobe moved from perpetual licenses to Creative Cloud subscriptions starting 2013. Revenue dropped for two years before recovering and exceeding the old model. The transition was painful, customer-hostile in the short term, and required board patience and balance-sheet strength. It worked because Adobe had cash and could absorb the dip.

The Class lesson: revenue-model changes (e.g., moving to consumption pricing, moving to multi-year, restructuring the renewal motion) take time and cost short-term revenue. Class probably *cannot* afford a major model change right now — there's no balance-sheet runway to absorb the dip. The honest answer: park the model-transition conversation for a year, focus on optimization within the current model.

### Slack 2014 — Customer-Led Repositioning After Glitch's Failure
Slack started as Tiny Speck's gaming company Glitch. When the game failed, Stewart Butterfield repositioned the internal team-chat tool as the product. The 2014 launch was preceded by an extended beta with hand-picked customers who shaped the product. The repositioning worked because it was *customer-led* — Slack listened intensely to early users and built around their actual workflows, not the team's vision.

The Class lesson: if Class needs to reposition (e.g., away from "synchronous video LMS" toward something like "EDU operations integration layer"), the repositioning should be customer-led. Identify 10-20 customers who love Class for reasons other than the obvious, interview them deeply, and let their language and use cases drive the new positioning. Resist the temptation to reposition based on what the leadership team finds intellectually appealing.

### Edtech Case 1 — Coursera's Pivot to Enterprise
Coursera started as consumer MOOCs, hit the wall when consumer free-rider economics didn't support the business, and pivoted hard to enterprise (Coursera for Business, Coursera for Government). The pivot saved the company, though the IPO has since traded poorly because the enterprise business hasn't grown as fast as needed.

The Class lesson: the institutional buyer (university, district, government agency) is where the real budget is in EDU. If Class is currently selling primarily to academic departments or individual faculty, there's a possible reframe to selling the whole institution. The risk: institutional sales cycles are long, and Class doesn't have time for an 18-month sales-cycle pivot. The play might be to *upsell* existing institutional customers rather than to fundamentally retool the sales motion.

### Edtech Case 2 — Instructure/Powerschool — PE-Led Rollup Dynamics
Both Instructure (Canvas) and Powerschool have been taken private by PE (Thoma Bravo and Bain/Vista respectively), with the explicit thesis of consolidating fragmented edtech. The PE playbook: acquire complementary assets at distressed prices, integrate ruthlessly, sell or IPO at multiple expansion. Many smaller edtech players in 2024-2026 are either being acquired into these rollups or going to zero.

The Class lesson: there are realistic strategic outcomes for Class that don't involve standalone survival. A sale to a PE-backed roll-up (or directly to Anthology, Instructure, or PowerSchool) might be the best risk-adjusted outcome for shareholders, employees, and customers. Russell should know the realistic acquirers, what they pay, what they look for in targets, and whether the current Class numbers fit. This is not defeatism — this is rational option-management.

## Section 5: Edtech & Class-Adjacent Market Patterns

### The 2024-2026 Edtech Contraction Cycle
Edtech is in a synchronized down-cycle driven by: ESSER funds ending (US K-12), Higher Ed enrollment declines, the ZIRP-era venture funding overhang (too many companies, too little revenue), and AI displacing point solutions. Companies that raised at peak 2021 valuations now face down-rounds or shutdowns. Public comps (Coursera, 2U, Chegg) trade at depressed multiples. M&A is the dominant exit, not IPO.

For Class: this means Class is not alone, and the macro is genuinely against the category. It also means buyers are scarce and prices are low. The strategic implication: time the market is not a strategy you can win; survive long enough to be one of the consolidators or one of the acquirees in a recovered market.

### Higher Ed Budget Crunch — Lag Effect on EDU SaaS
Higher Ed institutions are facing demographic decline (the "enrollment cliff"), state funding pressure, and increased operating costs. The lag effect on EDU SaaS: budgets that were committed in 2022-2023 are being renegotiated in 2025-2026. Renewal conversations now involve procurement, not just academic IT.

For Class: every renewal will be harder than the prior one. Multi-year deals get pressure-tested. Pricing comes under scrutiny. The mitigations: lock in multi-year commitments at favorable terms while you can, build relationships above the IT-buyer level (Provost, CFO), and have ROI stories that finance officers find credible.

### "AI Eats LMS" Thesis
The bear case for legacy LMS (Canvas, Blackboard, Brightspace, Moodle) and synchronous EDU video (Class, Zoom): AI-native platforms reframe what learning software does. Personalized tutors, AI-graded assessments, AI-generated content, real-time translation — all of these compress what the LMS used to do. The incumbents are racing to add AI features, but the architectural bet was made years ago and is hard to retrofit.

For Class: this is the existential question. Either Class becomes part of an AI-native education stack (likely as an integration partner or acquisition target) or Class becomes irrelevant. The play is to build the AI hooks that make Class indispensable as part of an AI-augmented workflow — not to compete with Khanmigo or Magic School on AI features.

### Collaboration Platform Commodification
Zoom, Teams, and Meet have absorbed most of the generic video conferencing market. The vertical-specific players (Class, Hopin/Ringcentral, Zoom Events) have all struggled. The remaining space for vertical players is narrow: deep workflow integration, regulatory compliance (FedRAMP, HIPAA, FERPA), or hardware-specific (telehealth, broadcasting).

For Class: pure "we're Zoom for education" is not a defensible position. The defensible position is "we're the EDU workflow layer that happens to include video." That reframe changes what's invested in (integrations, gradebook, attendance, FERPA compliance) and what's not (raw video quality, generic chat features).

### Federal Contracting — FedRAMP, GSA, ATO
Federal contracting is a $700B+ market with high barriers (FedRAMP authorization is $500K-$2M and 12-18 months) but high stickiness (once you're authorized and on a GSA schedule, federal procurement is sticky). For a SaaS company in distress, the question is: is the federal opportunity *now* worth the cost given finite cash, or is it a luxury for stable-state operations?

For Class: probably not now. FedRAMP investment burns cash that the company needs for survival. The exception: if a specific federal opportunity has line of sight to material revenue within 6-9 months and the authorization can be expedited (e.g., riding on an existing partner's authorization), revisit. Otherwise, park.

## Section 6: COO Operating Doctrines

### COO-CEO Partnership Patterns
The best COO-CEO partnerships have explicit role separation. Disney's Iger as COO to Eisner: Eisner external, Iger internal. Apple's Cook to Jobs: Jobs product/external, Cook operations/supply chain. Microsoft's Hood to Nadella: Hood finance/operations, Nadella vision/strategy. Stripe's Mahdavi to Collisons: brothers as founders/strategy, Mahdavi as operating execution. The pattern: clear division of labor, explicit decision rights, and a private "no surprises" cadence between the two.

For Class: Russell's relationship with Chasen needs to have explicit decision rights. Who decides on cuts? Who decides on board communications? Who fronts customer escalations? Without explicit clarity, every decision becomes a renegotiation and trust erodes. Russell should propose the operating model in writing within 30 days — Chasen can edit it, but having the artifact forces the conversation.

### Decision Rights — RAPID and DACI
RAPID (Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide) and DACI (Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed) are the two canonical frameworks. RAPID is more nuanced (the Agree role can veto, which clarifies authority). DACI is cleaner for fast-moving teams. Either works; the point is to *pick one* and use it consistently.

For Class: in a 41-person GTM org plus product, finance, and ops, the number of decisions that should reach the COO is small. Russell should run a quarterly decision-rights audit: list the top 20 decisions, name who decides each, name who is informed. The number-one COO failure mode is becoming a decision bottleneck. Push decisions down with explicit authority.

### The Operating Cadence — Monday Through Friday
The high-performing operating cadence: Monday morning leadership team meeting (alignment, top priorities for the week, top risks), Tuesday-Wednesday-Thursday execution days (no recurring meetings except essential 1:1s), Friday weekly review (what shipped, what slipped, top three for next week). Layered on this: weekly business review (metrics review), biweekly board chair update, monthly all-hands.

For Class: this cadence is appropriate, possibly tightened in a turnaround to include a daily cash huddle (15 minutes) and a weekly customer-save review. Resist the temptation to add more meetings; instead, make the existing meetings shorter and more rigorous.

### 1:1 Disciplines — Andy Grove's Structure
Grove's "High Output Management" lays out the 1:1 structure: it's the *report's* meeting, not the manager's; the report owns the agenda; the manager listens more than speaks; the meeting is about issues the report wants to surface, not status updates. Status goes in a written async update; the 1:1 is for what *isn't* in the written update.

For Class: Russell's 1:1s with direct reports should be 45-60 minutes weekly, report-owned agenda, status in writing in advance. The temptation in a turnaround is to make 1:1s into status meetings; that defeats the purpose. The actual purpose: surface what's stuck, what's worrying the report, what they need from you.

### Skip-Level Intel — Bezos
Bezos's discipline: regular skip-level meetings with people two levels below to ground-truth what's happening. Direct reports filter; skip-levels don't (yet). The discipline is to listen, take notes, never use anything against the direct report, and over time build a multi-layer picture of the org.

For Class: in a 40-person company, skip-levels can be done quickly. Russell should aim for a 30-minute skip-level with 10-15 people in the first 60 days. The intel from skip-levels is the *most accurate* source you'll get about what's actually broken.

### Team I Have vs Team I Need
The honest leadership question: if I were assembling this team today, with full knowledge of where we're going, who would I pick? The gap between "team I have" and "team I need" is the strategic personnel work. Don't confuse loyalty with capability — they're different.

For Class: Russell should do this assessment in the first 30 days, in writing, kept private. For each direct report: A-player, B-player, C-player. For each, is the gap fixable with coaching, with a different role, or only with replacement? The answer becomes the basis for the personnel-action calendar.

### Performance Management Under Decline
In normal times, you invest in marginal performers. In decline, you can't afford to. The threshold for "good enough" rises because the cost of a B-player in a critical seat is existential. Don't fire indiscriminately, but don't tolerate ambiguity either.

For Class: every quarter, ask "is this person delivering at the level we need given where the business is?" Honest answers, communicated honestly, with paths forward (clear expectations + coaching) or paths out (graceful separation). Avoid the trap of keeping people because firing them feels bad — the cost of carrying a B-player is paid by the A-players who notice.

## Section 7: AI-Native Operations Doctrine

### Where AI Is Highest-Leverage in a SaaS Turnaround
Highest-leverage AI applications, ordered by impact-per-dollar in a distressed SaaS context:

1. **Customer Support Automation**: AI deflection of tier-1 support tickets (Intercom Fin, Ada, custom Claude-based). For Class with 41 GTM, every hour of CS time freed is a real saving. Target 30-50% deflection on routine queries.
2. **Sales Prospecting and Outbound**: Apollo + Clay + AI personalization can replace SDR motion. Target: cut SDR comp/tooling by 50% while maintaining lead flow.
3. **Content Marketing**: Generate the long-tail SEO content, customer case studies, and thought-leadership cadence with AI as the first draft. Marketer becomes editor, not writer.
4. **Code Review and Engineering Productivity**: Cursor, Copilot, Claude Code in the engineering org. Productivity uplift of 20-40% on routine tasks.
5. **Customer Success Health Scoring**: AI-driven churn prediction and health scoring lets a smaller CS team manage a larger book without losing accounts.

For Class: pick the top 2 and do them well; resist the urge to do all five at once. The biggest near-term ROI is probably support automation (direct cost reduction) and sales prospecting (CAC compression).

### Build vs Buy AI Tooling
The default in 2026 is buy. Building AI infrastructure is mostly a distraction unless AI is your product. Buy: foundation-model APIs, SaaS AI tools (Intercom Fin, Apollo, Clay, Cursor, Gong). Build: the thin layer of integration that connects bought tools to your specific data and workflows.

For Class: zero AI infrastructure build. Every dollar spent training models or building proprietary tooling is a dollar not spent on survival. The exception: if Class's product itself becomes AI-driven (which it probably must, to be competitive in EDU), that's product investment, not ops investment, and is governed by different rules.

### AI-Native Team Structure — Centralize vs Distribute
Two patterns work. (1) Centralize: an "AI ops" function (1-2 people) that builds tools, evaluates platforms, and pushes adoption across functions. (2) Distribute: each function adopts AI tools native to it (sales picks Gong, support picks Fin, eng picks Cursor) with a light-touch coordinator.

For Class at 41 GTM + product + ops: probably distribute, with one named person (a current employee, not a new hire) who owns the cross-function coordination. A dedicated AI hire is premature.

### AI as Expensive Distraction vs Productivity Unlock
The failure mode: the org spends 30% of its time evaluating AI tools, building proofs of concept, and debating which platform to use. Meanwhile, the actual work doesn't get done faster. The discipline: pick a small number of tools, deploy fast, measure within 30 days, iterate.

For Class: a 30-day deployment timeline for any AI tool. If it's not delivering measurable improvement by day 30, kill it. AI tools that require six-month implementation cycles are not appropriate for a distressed SaaS.

### Measuring AI ROI in the First 90 Days
The metrics that matter: (1) Hours saved per week per function (track honestly with self-report + spot-check), (2) Headcount displacement enabled (does the AI tool let you not backfill an open role?), (3) Output velocity (more tickets closed, more outbound emails sent, more PRs merged). Resist soft metrics like "satisfaction" or "ease of use" — they don't pay the bills.

For Class: install the measurement framework before the tool. Pick 3 metrics per function, baseline them, deploy the tool, re-measure at day 30 and day 90. Anything that doesn't move the metrics gets killed.

### Specific AI Tools to Evaluate
- **Support**: Intercom Fin, Ada, Forethought
- **Sales**: Apollo, Clay, Gong, Outreach Smart Email
- **Marketing**: Jasper, Writer, Copy.ai, custom Claude-based content workflows
- **Engineering**: Cursor, Copilot, Claude Code, Sourcegraph Cody
- **Operations**: Glean (internal search), Notion AI, Airtable AI
- **Finance**: Numeric, Mosaic, custom Claude-based forecasting

Pick 1-2 per function based on actual use cases, not vendor pitches. Negotiate hard on pricing — vendors in 2026 are competing for accounts and will discount.

## Section 8: How to Use This Library

This library is a *reference layer*. When Claude is doing deep investigation for Russell, it should consult and cite this library explicitly. The disciplines:

**Cite frameworks by name.** When a question involves portfolio decisions, name BCG or Three Horizons. When it involves cash discipline, name 13-week and cite the doctrine. Named frameworks travel better in board conversations than ad-hoc reasoning.

**Apply case studies as analogies, not templates.** When proposing a strategic move, identify which case study it parallels (e.g., "this is the Apple 1997 subtraction play" or "this is the Best Buy human-side restructuring"), and explain why it applies *and* where it doesn't. Case studies are guides, not playbooks.

**Build conviction by citing.** Opinions backed by frameworks and historical precedent are credible. Opinions floating in air sound like guesses. When Russell takes a position to the board or to Chasen, the position should have a name and a precedent attached.

**Flag where conventional wisdom doesn't apply.** Class is in a specific situation (late-stage decline, edtech category contraction, cash-constrained, COO transition). Many famous frameworks assume conditions Class doesn't have. Be explicit when "this framework doesn't apply because..." That kind of nuance separates good thinking from cargo-cult thinking.

**Be opinionated.** This library is opinionated on purpose. Hedge less. The user is the COO of a dying SaaS company; he needs sharp positions to make sharp decisions. When a question has a clear answer in the doctrine above, give the clear answer.

**Update the library when you learn something new.** As deep investigations surface insights specific to Class (NRR data, segment-level economics, vendor-renegotiation outcomes), append them to the relevant section. The library should compound over time.

The goal: every Pass 1 bootstrap and every deep investigation has the full weight of turnaround doctrine, SaaS-specific patterns, edtech-specific context, and operating-discipline frameworks behind it. Russell shouldn't have to remember which framework applies — Claude should retrieve and apply.
